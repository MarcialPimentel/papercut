The short version

World

The Notebook Dojo: a feudal-Japan pop-up stage built from stationery. Graph paper floors, marble-cover mountains, legal-pad bamboo.

Fighters

Paper samurai with flat, angular, straight-edged shapes and a white sticker border. Every piece is cut card on a pin.

The red rule

The world is white, ink and paper colors. Red appears only where paper is cut: fighters are two-ply card with a red core.

Technology

Three.js flat shapes. The game draws each border itself, and cuts are real: the doll is sliced along the blade's line.

Making the art

Code-made dolls for Milestone 1. From Milestone 2, flat pieces drawn in Blender; the game adds borders, core and cuts.

Setting

Your references pull two ways: samurai duels (stern, angular, quiet) and school paper (graph paper, ruled sheets, legal pads, marble covers). I'd put the samurai on the paper rather than pick one. The duel stays serious; the materials carry the charm.

A · Recommended

The Notebook Dojo

A pop-up feudal Japan built from a kid's desk. The floor is a sheet of graph paper bent up into a backdrop. Mountains are cut from a marble notebook cover, the sun from a legal pad, clouds from ruled paper, gates from black card. Each arena is a different paper.

Why: it uses everything you sent, gives every arena a built-in identity at almost no art cost (the paper is the theme), and white fighters on ruled or grid paper read instantly. Cost: low. Most of it is generated textures and flat cut-outs.

B · Lower risk, less ours

Washi pop-up book

A traditional pop-up storybook: rice paper, ink-wash mountains, folded origami props. Same fighters and cuts.

Why not first: prettier on its own, but it's close to many existing papercraft games, it drops the stationery ideas you liked, and soft ink-wash backdrops fight with white fighters for attention.

Tone rules

  • Samurai film, school-desk materials. Characters are stern and quiet. The humour lives in the materials, never in mugging faces.
  • Straight lines only. Everything is cut with a blade: no curves anywhere. Even the sun and moon are cut as 16-sided polygons.
  • Red is paper, not blood. The red core is a material, so cuts read as dramatic, never gory.
  • Reference, not copy. Paper Mario, Samurai Jack, Kartana and South Park inspired the style. No characters, faces, logos or signature shapes are taken from them. No real brand names either: the marble cover is generic, with no label.
  • Japan as seasoning. Names, torii, katana, hakama. Any visible Japanese text gets checked by a native speaker before release.

No change of stack. The style fits Three.js unusually well: because the fighters are flat, a "real" cut is just a flat slice, which is cheap.

OptionVerdict
Three.js flat layered shapes + clipping-plane cuts RecommendedAlready the stack. Each piece is drawn as ink hairline, white border, red core and face, so borders are exact, and a cut slices along the blade's real line. It's what the mock-up above runs on.
Screen-space outline (post-processing)One border around the whole body, so it loses the per-piece paper margins that make it look cut out. It's also an extra render pass, and a white-plus-ink double border is fiddly. Keep it for later scenery touches at most.
Real-time 3D mesh cuttingNot needed: on flat dolls a clipping plane already is a true cut. Revisit only for folded 3D props.
2D engine (PixiJS or canvas)Loses the 3D pop-up diorama, real shadows and the rigging test-bed purpose of the game.
Rapier physics for falling halvesNot needed for Milestone 1. Scripted falls (as in the mock-up) look right and save about 1.5 MB. Add Rapier when pieces must bounce off scenery.

Loading

Under 2 MB to first duel. No image downloads in Milestone 1: every paper texture is generated in code.

Geometry

A doll is about 15 pieces of 6 flat layers, about 1,500 triangles. Whole scene under 20,000 triangles and 400 draw calls, even with Sumi cut in three.

Frame rate

60 fps on integrated laptop graphics, one 2048 shadow map. Hitstop and slow motion change game time only; input is read every frame.

Making the art

The style is built to be cheap: flat pieces with straight edges, two tones each, and the game adds the borders, the red core and the cuts. Drawing a new fighter means drawing about 15 flat shapes.

Milestone 1

Kami and Sumi built from code shapes (like the mock-up). Feel gets tuned on these.

Milestone 2

Kami redrawn in Blender: one flat mesh per piece, parented to a bone, exported as .glb. The game generates border, hairline and core from each piece's outline.

Later

Once the duel is fun, an illustrator can draw character sheets in this style and we trace them in Blender.

Making one fighter in Blender (preview)

  1. Add the concept image as a reference (Add › Image › Reference), viewed from the front.
  2. For each piece (head, hair, torso, arms, legs, feet, blade parts): Add › Mesh › Plane, then in Edit Mode move its corners to trace the piece. Straight edges only.
  3. Name each piece from the list (torso, head, armFU…).
  4. Add an armature with one bone per pin and parent each piece to its bone (Ctrl+P › Bone).
  5. Export with File › Export › glTF 2.0. A full click-by-click guide comes with Milestone 2.

Concept prompt

Original paper-doll samurai character, side view facing right, made of flat angular white card pieces joined at pins, clean straight razor-sharp edges, no curves, thick white sticker border with a thin black hairline outside it, black ink hair with a topknot, white gi, wide white hakama with light grey folds, indigo sash, katana with an indigo-wrapped grip, standing on blue graph paper, soft paper drop shadow, flat colors, no gradients, no text, no red anywhere.

Check the commercial-use terms of any image tool before using its output in the game, and don't name other franchises' characters in prompts.

What I need from you

  1. World: the Notebook Dojo (stationery pop-up, A), or the washi pop-up book (B)?
  2. The red rule: red appears only where paper is cut, because fighters are two-ply card with a red core. Agreed?
  3. Cuts: razor-clean cuts that slip (as in the mock-up) instead of torn edges?
  4. Outline: white sticker border with ink hairline, on fighters only (scenery gets no border)? Try the Outline button.
  5. Motion: smooth poses, or South Park-style stop-motion? Try the Motion button.
  6. Names: Kami for the player and Sumi for the rival?
  7. Rapier: leave it out of Milestone 1 and use scripted falls?

Risks: art is still the slowest part (the Blender step in Milestone 2 is the real test), and white-on-white can wash out, which is why every fighter keeps the ink hairline and a drop shadow.
