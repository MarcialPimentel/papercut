What changed in v4

The mouse fights. The cursor's height sets your guard (above the waist high, below low); left click is a light slash, right click a heavy. The keyboard only moves: A/D, Space to jump, double-tap to dash.
Feint. Cancel a heavy during its wind-up (left click or Q). Fake the heavy, bait their counter cut, punish the miss.
Riposte. Parry by flicking the cursor across the waist at the last moment; your next slash is a riposte: light speed, heavy damage, can't be parried.
No two players on one PC. PvP happens online (Milestone 2). Milestone 1 is a mouse feel test against the practice dummy, which now also feints.

The short version

World

The Notebook Dojo: a pop-up feudal Japan made of stationery, as a real 3D book. Each round is fought on a different spread.

Fighters

Folded papercraft samurai: low-poly, faceted, white, with a white sticker outline and an ink edge around every piece.

The red rule

Every fighter is a hollow paper shell, white outside and red inside. Red only appears where paper is cut: hits, the K.O. split, the health strip's cut edge.

The fight

Poke with lights, commit with heavies, guard high or low. Greed is fatal: anyone caught mid-attack is cut in half.

Technology

Three.js, no new stack. Moves defined as data on a fixed 60-frame clock, ready for rollback online. Rapier for the falling halves.

Making the art

Code-made fighters for Milestone 1, then low-poly models rigged in Blender. The game adds outlines, the red inside and cut marks to any model.

How a fight works

The mouse does all the fighting, in the style of PC melee games like Chivalry: where your cursor sits is where your blade guards, and the two buttons cut at that height. The light slash is the poke you throw all the time. The heavy slash is the hit that changes the round, and the risk that can end it for you.

Rounds

  • 60 seconds. A K.O. ends the round, or the most strip left when time runs out. A tie replays it.
  • Best of 3. Between rounds everything folds flat and the page turns to the next spread.
  • The match winner stands alone on the marble back cover.
  • Practice dummy: Sumi switches guard (a yellow glint is the tell), swings back now and then and sometimes feints a heavy, so you can practise counter cuts and parries alone.
  • Against people: online only (Milestone 2). No two players on one PC.

Where the depth comes from

Spacing at the tip of your light slash, reading the guard, feinting a heavy to bait their counter cut, parrying a predictable swing into a riposte, jumping a low, and the clock pressing on whoever is behind. Every round can turn on one greedy swing.

poke fast, never get greedy

Weapon fighters like Samurai Shodown inspired the heavy-hit feel, and PC melee games like Chivalry the mouse control, feint and riposte. The counter cut, the cursor guard and the paper damage are ours.

Fighters in 3D

Each body part is a low-poly folded shape (diamond-section torso and limbs, a faceted head, flared hakama legs), lit so every fold shows as a lighter and a darker facet. The game builds four extra layers for every part from the same model:

Ink edgeA slightly bigger copy, drawn inside out in ink: the thin outer hairline.
StickerA bigger copy drawn inside out in white: the sticker border, so every piece looks cut out.
Paper shellThe model itself, flat-shaded white (or the fighter's accent color).
Red insideThe shell's inside faces, plus a slightly smaller core, all in cut red. Invisible until a blade opens the shell.the red rule

A cut is a plane through the whole fighter along the blade's path. The game draws the fighter twice, once on each side of the plane, with the white layers stopping a hair short of it. A thin red line opens along the cut, and as the halves tumble apart you see straight into the red inside. Earlier hits stay on the halves, and halves stay on the page until it turns. A normal hit uses the same trick at a smaller scale: the struck part is redrawn in red, clipped to a thin band along the slash, so the cut wraps around the folds.

Readability stays the top priority: the camera sits side-on with a slight angle to show volume, every fighter keeps its white-and-ink outline against any paper, and the guard height is readable from the blade alone.

Tone rules

  • Samurai film, school-desk materials. Stern, quiet characters; the charm lives in the materials.
  • Straight lines and flat facets only. Everything is folded or cut with a blade: no smooth curves, even on the sun and moon.
  • Red is paper, not blood. The red inside is a material, so cuts read as dramatic, never gory.
  • Reference, not copy. Samurai Shodown, Paper Mario, Samurai Jack, Kartana and South Park inspired the feel and style; no characters, faces, logos, signature moves or systems are taken from them. No real brand names.
  • Japan as seasoning. Names, torii, katana, hakama. Any visible Japanese text gets checked by a native speaker before release.

Technology

No new stack. Three.js does the 3D, and nothing in the look needs custom shaders: flat shading, inside-out outline copies and clipping planes are all built in. A fighting game needs a few foundations from day one:

Fighting-game foundations

  • Fixed frames: the fight runs at exactly 60 steps a second, decided only by inputs, in plain code with tests.
  • Moves as data: wind-up, active and recovery frames, reach, damage, chip and stun for every move, all tunable live.
  • Hit areas: each move's blade reach is checked against the defender; jumping lifts you over low cuts.
  • Input buffer: a press a few frames early still counts, so the game feels responsive.
  • Mouse to guard: the cursor is compared with the fighter's waist on screen every frame; only the resulting high or low (and clicks) enter the rules, so online play sends the same tiny inputs.
  • Online (Milestone 2): only inputs travel between two browsers (WebRTC), with rollback to hide lag, the fighting-game standard.

Budgets

  • Loading: about 2 MB to the first fight; Rapier (about 1.5 MB) loads in the background.
  • Geometry: a fighter is about 15 parts, roughly 1,500 triangles with outlines. Scene under 30,000 triangles and 500 draw calls with halves on the page.
  • Frame rate: 60 fps on integrated laptop graphics, one 2048 shadow map. Hitstop and slow motion change game time only; input is read every frame.
OptionVerdict
Faceted shells + inverted-hull outline + clipping-plane cuts RecommendedWhat the mock-up runs on. Works on any model, including skinned Blender ones. Hit marks are the struck part redrawn in red, clipped to a thin band, so they wrap around the folds.
Decals or painted textures for hit marksFloat off folded facets or stretch; the clipped band follows the paper exactly.
Real mesh cutting (3D boolean)Slow, fragile on skinned meshes and hard to keep in sync online. Clipping looks the same from the game's camera.
Rapier physics for halvesChosen by Marz. Cosmetic only: halves bounce off pop-ups and pile up, never part of the rules. (The mock-up still uses scripted tumbling.)

Making the art

Low-poly on purpose: a fighter is about 15 simple folded shapes in one or two colors each. The game adds the outline, the red inside and the cut marks, so a model needs no special materials.

Later

New fighters and blades follow the same piece list, so each one is a modeling job, not a coding job.

One fighter in Blender (preview)

  1. Load the concept image as a side-view reference.
  2. Block each part from a low-poly primitive (a 4-sided cylinder for limbs and torso, a 6-sided one for the head). Keep facets big: no subdivision, no bevels.
  3. Set every part to flat shading and give it one plain color.
  4. Add an armature with one bone per part and parent each part to its bone.
  5. Export with File › Export › glTF 2.0. A full click-by-click guide comes with that milestone.
