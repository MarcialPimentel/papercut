The short version

World

The Notebook Dojo: a pop-up feudal Japan built from stationery, now a real 3D book. Each spread is a different paper: graph, ruled, legal pad, marble cover.

Fighters

Folded papercraft samurai: low-poly, faceted, white, with a white sticker outline and an ink edge around every piece.

The red rule

Every fighter is a hollow paper shell: white outside, red inside. A cut opens the shell, so red only appears where paper is cut.

The match

One clean cut kills. The winner gets the way and runs for the page edge. Turn the last page onto your rival's cover to win.

Technology

Three.js, no new stack. Faceted shells, an inverted-hull outline, clipping-plane cuts, and a fixed-step game loop ready for online play.

Making the art

Code-made fighters for Milestone 1. Then low-poly models rigged in Blender. The game adds the outline and the red inside to any model.

Fighters in 3D

Each body part is a low-poly folded shape (diamond-section torso and limbs, a faceted head, flared hakama legs), lit so every fold shows as a lighter and a darker facet. The game builds four extra layers for every part from the same model:

Ink edgeA slightly bigger copy, drawn inside out in ink: the thin outer hairline.
StickerA bigger copy drawn inside out in white: the sticker border, so every piece looks cut out.
Paper shellThe model itself, flat-shaded white (or the fighter's accent color).
Red insideThe shell's inside faces, plus a slightly smaller core, all in cut red. Invisible until a blade opens the shell.the red rule

A cut is a plane through the whole fighter along the blade's path. The game draws the fighter twice, once on each side of the plane, with the white layers stopping a hair short of it. A thin red line opens along the cut, and as the halves tumble apart you see straight into the red inside. Halves stay on the page until it turns, so a long fight leaves the spread littered with red.

Readability stays the top priority: the camera sits side-on with a slight angle to show volume, every fighter keeps its white-and-ink outline against any paper, and the guard height is readable from the blade alone.

Rules in the mock-up

  • One clean cut kills. The loser splits, then refolds a moment later ahead of the winner, standing in the way.
  • Whoever cut last has the way. Only they can run off the page edge toward the rival's cover. The page turns and the fight goes on.
  • Turn the last page and you win. The other player's cover closes the match.
  • Block: a slash meets a guard at the same height. Parry: switch to that height at the last moment and the attacker staggers.
  • Jump clears a low slash, or hops over a blocker to get past.
  • Clash: two slashes at the same height at the same moment bounce off each other.

Where the depth comes from

Few buttons, many reads: high or low, strike or wait for their wind-up, jump their low slash, fake a guard switch to bait a parry, or simply run when you have the way. Every exchange ends in one decisive moment, and a comeback is always one cut away.

easy to learn, high ceiling

The page-by-page push is a classic duel structure. Ours is told through the notebook: pages turn, covers close, and the red stays on the paper.

Tone rules

  • Samurai film, school-desk materials. Stern, quiet characters; the charm lives in the materials.
  • Straight lines and flat facets only. Everything is folded or cut with a blade: no smooth curves, even on the sun and moon.
  • Red is paper, not blood. The red inside is a material, so cuts read as dramatic, never gory.
  • Reference, not copy. Paper Mario, Samurai Jack, Kartana and South Park inspired the style; no characters, faces, logos or signature shapes are taken from them. No real brand names.
  • Japan as seasoning. Names, torii, katana, hakama. Any visible Japanese text gets checked by a native speaker before release.

Technology

No new stack. Three.js does the 3D, and nothing in the look needs custom shaders: flat shading, inside-out outline copies and clipping planes are all built in.

OptionVerdict
Faceted shells + inverted-hull outline + clipping-plane cuts RecommendedWhat the mock-up runs on. Works on any model, including skinned Blender ones. A cut is two clipped copies, and the red inside comes free from the shell's inner faces.
Screen-space outline (post-processing)One outline around everything, which loses the per-piece "cut out" look. It adds a full-screen pass and struggles with a white-plus-ink double edge.
Real mesh cutting (3D boolean)Gives true cut faces, but it's slow, fragile on skinned meshes and hard to keep in sync online. Clipping looks the same from the game's camera.
Toon shader with smooth modelsReads as cartoon, not paper. Folded facets are the identity.
Rapier physics for halvesScripted tumbling (as in the mock-up) is enough for Milestone 1. Rapier comes in when halves should bounce off pop-ups (decision 3).

Built for online from day one

  • The duel runs on a fixed 60-step-a-second clock, decided only by the players' button presses. The mock-up already runs this way.
  • Rules live in plain code with tests, separate from drawing, so the same match can be replayed exactly.
  • Milestone 2 sends only button presses between two browsers (WebRTC), with rollback to hide lag, plus a tiny service to connect players by invite link.
  • Effects (flecks, camera, halves tumbling) are cosmetic and never affect who wins.

Budgets

  • Loading: under 2 MB to the first duel; paper textures are generated in code.
  • Geometry: a fighter is about 15 parts, roughly 1,500 triangles with its outline copies. Scene under 30,000 triangles and 500 draw calls with halves on the page.
  • Frame rate: 60 fps on integrated laptop graphics, one 2048 shadow map. Slow motion and hitstop change game time only; input is read every frame.

Making the art

Low-poly on purpose: a fighter is about 15 simple folded shapes in one or two colors each. The game adds the outline and the red inside, so a model needs no special materials.

Later

New fighters and blades follow the same piece list, so each one is a modeling job, not a coding job.

One fighter in Blender (preview)

  1. Load the concept image as a side-view reference.
  2. Block each part from a low-poly primitive (a 4-sided cylinder for limbs and torso, a 6-sided one for the head). Keep facets big: no subdivision, no bevels.
  3. Set every part to flat shading and give it one plain color.
  4. Add an armature with one bone per part and parent each part to its bone.
  5. Export with File › Export › glTF 2.0. A full click-by-click guide comes with that milestone.

Concept prompt

Original low-poly papercraft samurai character, folded white paper with crisp flat facets like origami, three-quarter view, diamond-shaped torso and limbs, wide flared hakama trousers, black paper hair with a topknot, indigo sash, katana with an indigo-wrapped grip, thick white sticker outline with a thin black edge around each piece, standing on blue graph paper in an open notebook, soft drop shadow, flat colors, no text, no red anywhere.

Check the commercial-use terms of any image tool before using its output, and don't name other franchises' characters in prompts.
